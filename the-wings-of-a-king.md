# The Wings of a King

---

Before the castle, before the forge,
before the crown sat heavy on his brow,
the king had wings.

Not born with them—earned.
White wings, feathered and sure,
given by the bird people
who lived among the clouds.

He flew with them for eight long years,
crossing oceans, threading storms,
touching down in sixty-five cities
and two dozen kingdoms beyond his own.

He learned their ways:
how to calm a stranger's fear at thirty thousand feet,
how to carry grace through turbulence,
how to serve without surrendering yourself.

Once, on a mission across the sea,
he carried a child in his arms—
a girl born under a curse her elders believed,
marked for the desert, left to be forgotten.
But the bird people would not forget.
They sent the king and his companion
through Paris, past the old towers,
and over the Atlantic with two small lives in tow.

The girl clutched his shirt and would not let go.
Her tiny hands teaching him
what his heart already knew:
*he was meant to protect something.*

It was among the bird people
that he met *her*—
a woman with wings of her own,
sharp and beautiful,
who spoke the old tongues
and moved through the world
like wind through open doors.

They flew together.
They built [a nest](https://djangelic.com/the-nest.html).
Twig by twig, with patience and intention,
a home meant to shelter,
not to cage.

But the king,
so determined to make the nest unbreakable,
wove it tighter and tighter—
not knowing that what he meant as protection
she felt as walls.

And she—
restless, divided, building other nests
in places he could not see—
stopped bringing twigs home.
The threads that held them
unraveled quietly
until the nest collapsed
under the weight of what was missing.

---

Before the fall, the king made a choice.
He traded his wings for a forge.

The engineers' guild offered gold and steady ground—
a blacksmith's life, building invisible walls
to keep kingdoms safe from threats
they could not see.
He was paid well.
But he could not fly.

Then came the plague.

The skies went silent.
The bird people—his old companions—
were grounded, struggling,
their wings useless in a world
that had forgotten how to move.

But the king, safe in his castle,
hammer in hand, fire at his back,
worked on.
He had already given up the sky.
The plague could not take what he'd surrendered.

Still, in the quiet of those long months,
he watched the empty skies
and remembered what it felt like
to be weightless.

---

The nest fell. The forge burned hot.
What came next is written
in [other poems](https://djangelic.com/the-king-returns.html),
in [other fires](https://djangelic.com/forged-in-fire.html).

But a king who once had wings
does not forget the sky.

So he went to the dwarves.

Not the bird people—no.
The mountain folk, the metal shapers,
who work in iron and thunder
and do not care for feathers.

They forged him something new:
not wings of white,
but a *jet*—a dark machine,
a Nightster born of twelve hundred fires,
rumbling with the voice of the earth itself.

It did not glide. It *roared.*
It did not float above the world—
it cut *through* it,
close to the ground,
close to the danger,
close to the feeling of being alive.

The king rode it through the desert,
through the long Arizona roads
where the horizon bends like a bowstring,
and for the first time since he lost his feathers,
he felt the wind again.

Not the same wind.
A different kind of flight.
Closer to the earth, closer to the fire,
but flight all the same.

---

Then came the fall.

Not from the sky this time,
but from the road.
Metal against pavement,
the machine wounded,
the king walking away
with scars that matched the ones inside.

The jet sits still now.
Waiting in the forge for the dwarves to mend it.
The king passes it some mornings
and remembers the sound it made
when the desert opened before him
like a promise.

He has not ridden since.

Not because he cannot.
Not because the road has changed.
But because courage,
like a blade left in the rain,
needs to be cleaned and sharpened
before it cuts true again.

He will call the dwarves this week.
He will schedule the mending.
And when the machine is whole,
he will stand beside it
and decide—not if,
but *when*—
to fly again.

Because a king who has known the sky,
who has carried children across oceans,
who has built nests and watched them fall,
who has been forged and broken and forged again—

that king does not stay grounded forever.

He rises.
He always rises.

---

*And each morning, he tends the castle.*
*And each evening, he closes the gates.*
*Not because he must,*
*but because a kingdom kept with care*
*is a kingdom worth returning to*
*when the wings are ready again.*
